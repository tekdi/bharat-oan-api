## Interview Transcript (Round 1 — Non-Tech, 50 min)

**Interviewer:** Walk me through a project you had real ownership of — what were you responsible for day to day?

**Arjun:** The citizen services project at GovTech is the clearest example. I was the single point of accountability on the client side — everything from weekly governance calls with the PS's office to managing the vendor teams on the ground. Day to day that meant: morning standups with tech leads, escalation calls whenever a vendor was blocked, and a weekly status report I wrote and presented myself.

**Interviewer:** What was the hardest part of managing that project?

**Arjun:** The hardest thing was that three government departments each thought they were the primary stakeholder, and they had conflicting priorities for the same features. The health department wanted a particular grievance field that the revenue department said was unnecessary and would confuse their officers. I had to navigate that without making anyone feel overruled.

**Interviewer:** What specifically did you do?

**Arjun:** I stopped trying to resolve it in the governance meeting — that was just creating public disagreements. I started meeting each department head separately the week before, understanding their actual concern, and by the time we got to the joint meeting I could usually reframe it as "the health department needs X for Y reason, can we accommodate that as an optional field" rather than a conflict. It didn't always work, but it got us moving.

**Interviewer:** Scenario — you've taken over a project that's 3 weeks behind schedule. Client knows and is unhappy. Team of 4. Walk me through the first 48 hours.

**Arjun:** First thing I'd do — not send an update to the client. That's the instinct but it's the wrong move until I understand what I'm talking about. First 3-4 hours: understand the actual state. Not the PM's summary of the state — sit with each person on the team, understand what they're working on, what's blocking them, what they think the real issue is.

Then I'd want to know: is this a scope problem, a resource problem, or an execution problem? Those need different responses. If it's scope — the client added work that was never in the plan — that's actually a conversation I need to have with them. If it's execution — we're just behind — then I need to understand why before committing to any recovery timeline.

Only after that would I talk to the client. I'd be honest about where we are and give them a specific date by which I'll have a recovery plan, not just reassurances.

**Interviewer:** What's something you've gotten noticeably better at in the last two years?

**Arjun:** Saying no — or at least "not yet" — to scope creep. Early on I used to try to absorb everything because I didn't want conflict. I learned it doesn't help anyone. The client ends up with a delayed project and a team that's burning out. Now I'm much more explicit about what goes into a formal change and what doesn't, and I'll raise it in the governance meeting even if it's uncomfortable.

**Probe — accountability:** Tell me about a project or deliverable that didn't land the way you intended.

**Arjun:** The rural healthcare project at Wipro. The requirements document I produced was accurate but not useful — I'd done thorough stakeholder interviews but I wrote the output in a way that worked for the tech team but not for the government officers who needed to sign off on it. They didn't understand it, approvals took 6 weeks longer than they should have, and it pushed back the whole downstream timeline. My mistake — I should have validated the format with the actual decision-maker before circulating it.
